Lesson 0003

Mercor AI interview: the 15-minute mock

Rehearse the actual interview out loud — background, research reasoning, and logistics — against model answers and a scoring rubric.

How this fits your mission

You applied to Mercor's Generalist (MacBook) project: $50–60/hr, 10–20h/week, weekly pay via Stripe or Wise. The pipeline is: resume → 15-minute AI interview → paid assessment → decision.

This lesson is the interview rehearsal. Run it out loud, not in your head — the interviewer is scoring spoken clarity and reasoning, and silent prep doesn't transfer.

What the AI interviewer is actually grading

  • Structure — do your answers have a shape, or do they wander?
  • Reasoning aloud — can the evaluator hear how you think, not just the conclusion?
  • Research judgment — do you instinctively check sources, decompose claims, verify?
  • English fluency — complete sentences, moderate pace, precise vocabulary.

The answer format: Claim → Reasoning → Example. State your position, explain the thinking, ground it in something real. Almost every strong answer in this mock follows it.

The mock — run it like the real thing

Set a 15-minute timer. For each question: answer out loud first, then open the model answer and compare. Don't read the model before speaking — that's fluency, not storage.

Round 1 — Background (certain to be asked)

Q1. "Walk me through your background."

Say it out loud. Target: 60–90 seconds. Then compare.

Model answer (adapt it — don't memorize)

"I'm a software engineer with about three years of industry experience. I came through Morocco's preparatory classes — the CPGE system — and then ENSIAS, the national engineering school for computer science. Since then I've worked as a full-stack engineer, mainly PHP and Laravel on the backend, JavaScript on the front, and I've shipped side projects on my own time — most recently a personal productivity platform I built end to end. I'm drawn to this role because it uses the analytical side of engineering — evaluating, reasoning, writing precisely — and it's flexible enough to run alongside my main work."

Why it works: arc (education → career → now), names real credentials, ends by connecting to the role. No rambling, no filler.

Round 2 — Research & reasoning (the real test)

Q2. "How would you research whether a factual claim is true?"

Narrate your process out loud — they're scoring the process, not the answer.

Model answer

"First I'd decompose the claim into the specific assertions it's making — vague claims can't be verified. Then I'd go to primary sources wherever possible: official documents, original papers, the data itself, rather than articles summarizing them. I'd check credibility — who published it, when, what's their incentive. If two credible sources conflict, I'd look at their methodology before picking one. Finally I'd state the conclusion with the confidence level the evidence supports — 'confirmed,' 'likely,' or 'unverifiable' are all valid outcomes."

Scoring points: decompose → primary sources → credibility → conflict handling → calibrated conclusion.

Q3. "Tell me about a time you researched something complex and had to verify conflicting information."

Use a real story — yours already exists. Think STAR: Situation → Task → Action → Result.

Model answer (built from your real experience)

"When I started planning to relocate to Europe, I needed to understand the Netherlands' 30% ruling — a tax advantage for skilled migrants. Sources conflicted: forums described the old rules, while the allowance had been cut back. So I went to the official Belastingdienst documentation for the current rules, cross-checked against recent expat guides to see how they applied in practice, and separated 'current law' from 'historical discussion.' The result was a concrete picture of eligibility and net-salary impact that I built my financial plan around."

Why it works: real stakes, shows source hierarchy (official docs > forums), and the conflict-resolution method is explicit.

Q4. "What makes a good research question?"

Model answer

"A good research question is specific, answerable, and has a defensible conclusion — one a knowledgeable person could verify or refute. Weak: 'Is AI dangerous?' — too broad, no ground truth. Strong: 'What did the EU AI Act's 2024 provisions change about foundation-model disclosure requirements?' — checkable, scoped, and the answer exists in primary documents. The test I use: could two careful researchers independently produce the same answer?"

Q5. "How do you evaluate whether an AI-generated answer is good?"

Model answer

"Four things, in order: accuracy — are the facts and logic actually correct; grounding — does it cite or reflect real sources rather than plausible-sounding claims; completeness — does it cover what the question requires; and relevance — did it answer the question that was asked, not an adjacent one. A fluent, well-formatted answer that's wrong is worse than an ugly correct one — confident wrongness is the main failure mode to catch."

Round 3 — Motivation & logistics (don't fumble the easy points)

Q6. "Why this role?"

Model answer

"The work sits exactly on my strengths — research, evaluation, and precise writing — and the flexible structure lets me do it properly alongside my main job rather than squeezing it in. Longer term, contract work like this is part of my plan to build experience with international teams."

Q7. Logistics — MacBook model? Availability? Start date?

Have the exact answers ready. Open About This Mac on the machine beforehand.

Model answer

"I have access to a MacBook [Air/Pro] with an M[1/2/3] chip running macOS [version]. I can commit [realistic number] hours per week — evenings and weekends — and I can start immediately."

Memorize the chip and OS version exactly — hesitating on the one hard requirement looks bad.

Checkpoint — which intro is strongest?

Three candidates answer "walk me through your background." Which is strongest?

Self-score after the mock

For each answer, check honestly:

  • ⬜ Did it have a shape (claim → reasoning → example)?
  • ⬜ Did you say it out loud without long silent stalls?
  • ⬜ Complete sentences, no heavy filler?
  • ⬜ Intro under 90 seconds?
  • ⬜ Did research answers show process, not just a conclusion?

5/5: run the mock once more tomorrow for storage strength, then go do the real thing. 3 or fewer: redo the weakest round — out loud — before the interview.
